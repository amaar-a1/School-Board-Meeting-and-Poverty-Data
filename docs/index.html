        About this map: borders and special cases, country by country

        District borders are the Census Bureau's 2024 cartographic boundaries (simplified). Unified and elementary districts are shown; in states that also have separate high school districts, tick “Secondary districts” to draw those on top. Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and American Samoa have one territory-wide district each, so their outline is the whole territory. Vermont's poverty figures are for supervisory unions, so each Vermont town district shows its union's figures (CivicSearch has no Vermont boards; meetings counted from other sites cover only a few unions, and others may post meetings elsewhere); districts merged in 2024 show the merged district's figures on their old borders. High school pieces of districts show their parent district. Districts the Census poverty file leaves out (Defense Department base schools, districts with no schools of their own, a few tiny districts) show their county's all-ages poverty rate, noted in their tooltip. Districts with no meetings found count as 0 (palest blue). Pale green areas are outside any school district, mostly national parks and other federal land. When a state is picked or filters are on, other districts are faded.

        Canada (pick “Canada” above): school board borders come from each province (British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario and Quebec publish them). Nova Scotia, New Brunswick and the Northwest Territories boards are drawn from the counties or regions they serve. Boards that serve a whole province or territory (French-language boards, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Yukon and Nunavut) are drawn as the whole province or territory. Canada's boards overlap: public and Catholic boards, and English and French ones, serve the same places. The map shows the public (or English) board for each place by default (in the Yukon, the Yukon First Nation School Board; in Nunavut, its French board, the only board there); tick “Overlapping Canadian boards” to draw the Catholic and French-language boards on top. Boards with no meetings found are drawn too, in the palest blue. Land outside every public board (most of Ontario's Far North: unorganized land and First Nations reserves, whose schools are run by First Nations or small school authorities that publish no borders) is drawn as “Rest of Ontario (no school board)”, and likewise “Rest of Alberta” (mostly national parks and First Nations reserves). Their rate comes from the census areas inside them (Statistics Canada has no low-income figures for reserves, so it mostly reflects unorganized areas), or the province-wide rate where there are too few children; meetings are 0. These are on the map only, not in the provinces' tables, charts or totals. Canada's colors use the share of children 0–17 in low income (2021 Census, LIM-AT), which isn't the same measure as the US child poverty rate.

        School board meetings only

        Many countries have no elected school board, so this site often counts a stand-in: the town council's meetings, or a cantonal or territorial parliament's. This tab leaves the stand-ins out and counts only meetings of a school board or its closest equivalent: school boards (the US, Canada, Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands), Ireland's Education and Training Boards, UK councils' education and children's committees, municipal education or school committees (where a country's councils record them), and Japan's boards of education. Places whose only meetings are a stand-in count as having none here.

          Does poverty predict whether meetings are posted?

          How to read it. For each country, the places are compared with others of the same size there (by population, or enrollment where there's no population), since bigger places post far more meetings. Odds ratio is how the odds of posting any meetings at all compare between the poorest place in the country and the richest, from a logistic regression on the place's poverty rank in its country and the log of its size: below 1 means poorer places are less likely to post meetings, above 1 more likely. The dot and line show it and its 95% range on a scale from a hundredth to a hundred times; a line that crosses the middle (1) means no clear link. p is the chance of a link at least that strong if there were none; under 0.05 counts as a link here, and “weak” marks one that doesn't hold after allowing for testing every country at once (a Bonferroni correction). Rank corr. (Spearman's rank correlation) compares poverty with the number of meetings, without allowing for size. Places with an estimated poverty rank are left out, and so are countries with one poverty figure for every place, or too few places with (or without) meetings. The highlighted row is the country you're viewing.

          What it doesn't show. A link isn't proof that poverty causes it: poorer places are often also more rural or less connected. “Posted” means a recording or record could be found online, not that a meeting was held, and in many countries the meetings counted are a council's or parliament's, standing in for a school board's (see each country's note). Poverty is measured differently in every country, which is why each place is ranked only within its own. And neighbouring places aren't independent of each other, so the p-values for big countries are somewhat optimistic.               How to read this site

              This site puts two things side by side for school districts, councils and other local areas in many countries: how poor each place is, and how many of the meetings that govern its schools have been posted online. The question behind it is whether poorer places are less open about how their schools are run.

              Pick a country, then a view

              The menu at the top picks a country, or All for everywhere at once; the second menu, in some countries, picks a state, province or region. Show picks a view: the Overview (summary, chart and the full table), the Map, Trends over the years, and Compare two places; views that group places by size, locale or density; the Statistics; and this page, the rules and the sources. Share copies a link to exactly what's on screen, or the code to embed a map or chart.

              Countries aren't directly comparable

              Every country measures poverty its own way: a child poverty rate in the US, the share of households below a poverty line in Malaysia, a multidimensional index in India and Mexico. So a figure from one country can't be set against another's. To compare across countries, use each place's poverty rank in its country (0 is the least poor place there, 100 the poorest); the Statistics view works only with ranks for this reason.

              Stand-ins

              Where a country publishes no poverty rate for local areas, the site uses the closest published figure as a stand-in: a benefit or welfare rate, an unemployment rate, or a low-income share. Its tooltip and the country's note say so. Meetings have stand-ins too: most countries have no school boards, so the meetings counted are those of the council that runs or oversees local schools, such as a town council or a district assembly. The School boards only view and the map's checkbox of the same name leave stand-in meetings out.

              Estimates

              Some figures are estimates, not counts: enrollment where no pupil count is published (the school-age children living there, from census or WorldPop age data), and a few poverty figures borrowed from a wider area. Each is marked in its tooltip, and ticking No estimates on the map greys them out.

              Zero meetings doesn't mean no meetings

              Meetings were found by searching each place's council or board channel on YouTube (and CivicSearch for US districts). Many councils post only on Facebook or their own website, or don't record at all, so a 0 means none were found online where the site looked, not that none were held.

              Data quality labels

              Under each country's note are three coloured labels: solid, use with care and weak, for its poverty figure (what kind, how old, how many places have their own), its enrollment (counted or estimated) and its meetings (a school board's or a stand-in's). The Sources tab has the table for every country.

              Reading the map

              Pick what to colour by: poverty or its rank; meetings, meetings per year (fairer to councils that began posting recently), meetings against same-size places, or time since the latest one; enrollment, public schools' share, locale or density. Under Over time, colour by poverty, enrollment or public schools' share in a chosen year (pick the year, or press Play to step through them), or by how poverty, enrollment, population or public schools' share changed between the earliest and latest years published. Several years are published for the US (child poverty, 2019–2024), Czechia, Malaysia, Indonesia, Paraguay, Spain, Mexico, the Nordic countries, the UK and a few others. Hover or tap a place for its figures. Grey means no figure. The map loads detailed borders as you zoom in.

              Reading the statistics

              The Statistics view asks, country by country, whether poorer places are less likely to post any meetings than places of the same size there. A link isn't proof that poverty causes it, and it says nothing about whether meetings were held, only whether they were posted where the site looked. The view explains each figure.

              Using the data

Some sources update automatically: US meeting counts daily, and some countries' figures and meeting counts monthly; the rest are as of the date in each country's footer.
